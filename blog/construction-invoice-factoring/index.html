Construction Invoice Factoring in Australia: A Full Guide for Builders and Subcontractors

Zap Funds | 14 August 2026

Most construction companies do not have a problem of having sales, they have a problem of the timing. When you finish a project you invoice and then - 45 days - 60 days - sometimes 90 days - after that. The wages go out every other Thursday and the subcontractors want cash in 30 and the fuel card never stops.

If your business is in construction and you think these figures are depressing, then you will understand why invoice factoring serves this niche.

However, for all this the industry employs around 1.3 million of Australia's workforce across 487,000 companies and contributes around 7% to Australian GDP - and as such is the single biggest industry for insolvency in the country - ASIC recorded 3,600 insolvencies in 2024-25 or 26% of all insolvency activity in Australia for a single industry (that is more than three times as much again than the 2021-22 equivalent) and a report on the ASIC data showed that the industry analysis suggests they are in fact three-quarters of those failures and as such it is subcontractors bearing the brunt of this.

The payment problem, in numbers

CreditorWatch also reveals how long Australian businesses take to pay each other and construction languishes at the lowest end of the scale. At around 1.8 to 1.9%, construction has the highest default rate in any industry group by far and the proportion of outstanding invoices over 60 days has risen to 6.5%, the highest level of any industry since the start of 2018. The delay at the top of a job becomes a cash flow problem three tiers below by default when contractors pass on their problems to their suppliers and so on. On average contractors make their suppliers wait about 44 days to pay them, which is not great.

Costs also have not come down either. The cost of building materials from statistics provided by ABS (Australian Bureau of Statistics) is around 40% more than in the first half of 2020. This trend is predicted to remain above the rate of normal inflation.

Another statistic; invoice finance in Australia is hardly utilised in comparison to a country such as the UK, with nearly 19% of GDP being through invoice finance compared to 4% in Australia. However, the IMARC report forecasts this figure is set to double over the period to 2034, so clearly the sector still has much room to grow.

So what is it?

You then give the invoice to a finance provider, who look at the invoice and then your customer to see if it makes sense and then give you much of the money from the invoice. When your customer pays, they deduct their charges and pass the remaining on to you.

Suppose you are a sparky and you supply a building contractor an invoice for $80,000. The invoice was issued 45 days ago and you still have your invoice money on a Thursday and so does the building contractor. If you want money today from that invoice you may be in for something with a company known as "factoring" but not on the strength of the invoice alone and this will require the factoring company checking your invoice out before paying the cash.

How it runs, step by step

  1. You do the actual construction whether that be fit-out, plumbing, civil work or tiling or whatever the job is.
  2. You invoice: usually under your state's Security of Payment laws - which means, for construction work, it involves issuing a progress claim in the correct manner. This is worth doing properly because it will come into play later on.
  3. Then you pass all this through backed up by the contract, orders, deliveries and certificates.
  4. Accepted - You are paid upfront (a proportion of the invoice)
  5. Your customer will pay.
  6. Pay the charges and then a refund is issued.

What it looks like in dollars

A commercial plumber does some work for a builder on an invoice for $100,000, pay terms are 45 days and so he wishes to factor that invoice. This is an example of an 80% advance.

Outstanding invoiceThis amounts to $100,000

That is 80 grand on Friday morning, not six weeks. The interest, cost and terms will vary depending on what company you use. This just shows how a typical advance might look.

Why construction cash flow is brutal

But it is a business that often sees payment for several months before seeing its first dollar. If three jobs are being carried out all at the same time then a receivables pile quickly builds up. This business does not look kindly to wages and super just because of a payment delay on the developer's side. You pay a concrete supplier every couple of weeks but you get a 60-day credit from the builder. Equipment hire and fuel costs all the time. And if a progress claim is challenged, you are going to be running on all cylinders. What it ends up looking like for the insolvency statistics if it collapses is this.

The Security of Payment laws worth knowing

Each country and state of Australia has its own Security of Payment legislation e.g. Queensland has the Building Industry Fairness (Security of Payment) Act 2017 whereas Building and Construction Industry Security of Payment Act 1999 (the Security of Payment legislation that has been recently amended in 2020) governs NSW. The legislation will be slightly different from state to state however the structure and main features will always remain consistent.

This right to advance claims arises by virtue of law, regardless of any reference to this in the contract.

They must serve a payment schedule (which is a statement of their intention to pay and the reasons why), usually within 10 business days of receiving the claim.

Not happy with the settlement offered? Bring it to an adjudication. This should be a relatively swift process, with a decision generally reached within 10 working days.

In 2020 in response to this, the "pay-when-paid" loophole was closed and head contractors cannot use this as an excuse for delaying payments.

Retention (trust money) in NSW and Queensland subcontractors can look to the retention trust as the head contractor goes under. (Project bank accounts are available for government contracts).

All this means to you when it comes to factoring, is that a properly notice of claim that can be supported by an adjudication certificate, would be simple for the lender to accept; an informal claim that the builder wishes to reject in hushed tones, would not be.

Who uses it

Invoicing commercial clients - any builder or subcontractor. Electricians, roofers, plumbers, concreters, excavators, civil crews, scaffolders, HVAC, steel fabrication and even tilers, glaziers, painters, landscapers, waterproofers, insulators, floorers, demolition crews and so on. As long as you invoice and get paid at some stage later, you are in the game, regardless of who you are working for. The question will always be: Is the invoice genuine and will it ever be paid by the client.

Subcontractors, progress claims and retention

It is the obvious area of business where this type of finance would be appropriate - last link in the payment chain and also the most vulnerable with the largest proportion of sector insolvencies.

Can you fund a progress claim? That depends on what the claim is for. If it is a straightforward claim, then the factor should be able to pay that for you. As the factor will be requesting proof that the work has been completed and proof that the claim has been served properly. They need to know it is a genuinely owed amount. However if the claim is for a variation or any other unaccepted claim then things become more difficult.

Retention is another area with problems, normally about 5% held by the subcontractor which is not paid until practical completion or after defects and so you will have someone's money on deposit for a long period (sometimes a year) and if the head contractor goes under and you may have to get that money out via liquidation! Many standard factoring schemes cannot deal with this until the cash is owed and is therefore not always covered.

Factoring or a bank loan?

A loan is a lump sum you pay back over a fixed period and based on your finances, collateral and so on while a line of credit will take your invoices as its collateral. The amount increases as your business increases and the lender takes more interest in what your clients do not your home. As you can see there is no absolute answer and it is down to the circumstances in which you want to borrow and what your finances are like.

The other piece of info that may be interesting is that the new SME loans made in Australia have jumped from $122 billion in 2023 to $154 billion in 2024 (OECD data). The banks are lending; they are certainly lending to construction companies whose balance sheets are pretty anaemic.

Before you sign anything, ask

  • What is the cost at the higher rate in a 60-day period?
  • Do you accept progress claims? Certified / Adjudicated Only?
  • How should you treat retention?
  • Recourse or non-recourse? Who will wear the risk if the customer fails to pay?
  • "Will my customers know? "
  • What if they pay too late or do not pay at all?

Common questions

Can small builders and contractors use QuickBooks? If you do supply invoices to businesses or customers and they meet the criteria of the provider then you sure can.

What happens if the customer does not pay? Depends on the facility; 'recourse' means they can come for you and 'non-recourse' means it will protect some of the credit risk associated with the customer (but always read the small print).

Will not it cost too much to finance your work? Different financing companies have differing rates depending on the value of the invoice, length of payment period and how risky the customer is. Compare them to your cost of employing the builder for two months.

Will this impact my business credit?A question worth asking and the answer can literally vary from provider to provider, so best check on application.

Do I need property security? It is not really necessary (the receivables themselves constitute 'security') but not all suppliers would accept such an answer.
