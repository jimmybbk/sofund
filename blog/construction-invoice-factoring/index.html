Construction Invoice Factoring in Australia: A Full Guide for Builders and Subcontractors

Zap Funds | 21 November 2026

Construction invoice factoring helps Australian builders, subcontractors and civil contractors get hold of money stuck in invoices without waiting for their customers full payment terms.

The cash flow stakes in this industry are high. Construction gives work to around 1.3 million Australians across 487,000 businesses and brings in roughly 7% of national GDP, yet it always records the highest corporate insolvency rate of any industry. In 2024-25, ASIC data shows construction made up 26% of all corporate insolvencies: approximately 3,596 companies, the sectors highest annual figure on record and nearly triple the level of 2021-22.

Projects need spending on labour, materials, equipment and subcontractors, while customers may take 30, 60 or even 90 days to pay. Factoring bridges that gap by letting eligible businesses get an agreed percentage of invoices before customers pay.

The Numbers Behind the Payment Problem

Recent Australian data tells a story:

  • Record insolvencies. Construction led all industries in 2024-25 and industry analysis of ASIC data suggests subcontractors make up three-quarters of those failures.
  • Slow, worsening payments. CreditorWatch reports construction has the trade payment default rate of any industry (recently around 1.8-1.9%), with around 6.5% of construction invoices more than 60 days overdue, the worst levels since 2018. Construction firms were themselves paying suppliers 44 days late on average, so delays roll down the supply chain.
  • Cost inflation. ABS data shows building material costs remain 40% higher than in early 2020.
  • Underused finance. Invoice finance volumes sit at 4% of Australian GDP versus close to 19% in the UK, even as analysts such as IMARC expect the local factoring market to roughly double between 2025 and 2034.

What Is Construction Invoice Factoring?

It's a type of invoice finance where a provider pays out funds against unpaid customer invoices. The provider looks at the invoice, the customer responsible for payment and the underlying transaction. When the customer pays, the provider settles the transaction. Releases the balance after fees.

For example, an electrical subcontractor invoices a builder for $80,000 with 45-day terms. While waiting, it still has to pay electricians, buy supplies and cover vehicle costs. Factoring can give it access to much of that $80,000 subject to approval. Providers do not automatically accept every invoice.

How It Works

  1. Complete the contracted work, covering electrical, plumbing, civil, concreting, roofing or general building.
  2. Issue the invoice, as a compliant progress claim under your states Security of Payment legislation.
  3. Submit for assessment with contracts, purchase orders, delivery confirmations, certifications or adjudication outcomes.
  4. Receive the advance, an agreed percentage of the invoice amount.
  5. Customer pays the invoice.
  6. Balance released after fees.

Worked example

A commercial plumbing contractor invoices $100,000 on 45-day terms. Applies for factoring. Assume an 80% advance:

Why Cash Flow Is So Hard in Construction

Contractors spend for a time before they get paid and the pressure builds up: extended payment terms mean receivables pile up across projects running at the same time; wages, superannuation and workers compensation are due no matter what the customer pays; materials must often be bought on 14- or 30-day supplier terms while customers pay in 45-90 days; and equipment, fuel and hire costs never pause. When a progress claim is queried or disputed, the wait gets longer. The industrys insolvency statistics show what happens when contractors can't take that shock.

Australias Security of Payment Laws

Every state and territory has Security of Payment legislation, such as the Building and Construction Industry Security of Payment Act 1999 (NSW, amended 2020) and the Building Industry Fairness (Security of Payment) Act 2017 (QLD). The core features:

  • right to progress claims, regardless of what the contract says about payment.
  • Fast response deadline. Customers disputing a claim must generally provide a payment schedule within 10 business days.
  • Expedited adjudication. A low-cost determination process with decisions due in around 10 business days.
  • Conditional payment restrictions. NSWs 2020 reforms stop "pay-paid" style set-offs against subcontractors.
  • Trust protections. NSW requires subcontractor retention to be held in trust and Queensland subcontractors can claim directly against retention trusts if a head contractor becomes insolvent. Project bank accounts ring-fence funds on government projects.

Why it matters for factoring: served claims, especially those certified, admitted or supported by an adjudication determination, are much easier for a finance provider to assess and fund than informal or contested amounts.

Which Businesses Can Benefit?

Any contractor issuing invoices: commercial builders, electrical and plumbing contractors, civil construction companies, concreters, excavators, roofers, steel fabricators, scaffolders, HVAC contractors, plus painters, demolition, landscaping, fit-out, tiling, glazing, waterproofing, insulation, flooring, security, fire protection, formwork and steel fixing businesses. The key test is whether your invoices and customers meet the providers requirements.

Subcontractors, Progress Claims and Retention

Subcontractors sit at the end of the payment chains and make up the majority of construction insolvencies, so they're a natural fit for factoring, particularly when working with established commercial customers.

Progress claims can sometimes be financed,. Ordinary factoring doesn't automatically cover every claim. Providers typically want evidence the work was completed, the claim was properly. The amount is payable under the contract. Disputed variations and uncertified claims are harder to fund.

Retention, 5% of each subcontract sum held until practical completion or the end of the defects liability period, ties up working capital for a year or longer and can be lost if an upstream contractor fails. Retention is generally not eligible for factoring because it isn't yet presently payable; check with providers about specialist options.

Factoring vs Traditional Business Loans

Loans give a fixed amount with scheduled repayments, assessed on financials, assets and credit history. Factoring is linked to invoices, so funding grows with your project pipeline and assessment focuses on receivables and customer risk rather than property security. Neither is automatically better; the right choice depends on your position and what the funds are for. It's a comparison: OECD data shows new lending to Australian SMEs rose from about AUD 122 billion in 2023 to AUD 154 billion in 2024, but construction businesses with thin balance sheets often struggle to get conventional credit.

Questions to Ask Before Applying

  1. What percentage of invoices can be advanced. At what cost?
  2. Are progress claims accepted. Must they be certified or adjudicated?
  3. How are retentions treated?
  4. Is the facility recourse or non-recourse?
  5. Is customer notification required?
  6. What happens if a customer pays late or disputes a claim?

Frequently Asked Questions

Can small builders and subcontractors use it? Yes, if they issue qualifying invoices to customers and meet the providers assessment requirements.

What if my customer doesn't pay? Under recourse facilities you may remain responsible for invoices; non-recourse facilities may protect against specified customer credit risks, subject to conditions.

Is factoring expensive? Costs vary by provider, amount, payment period and customer risk. Compare cost and weigh it against the cost of funding payroll and materials through 60-plus-day delays.

Does factoring affect my business credit? It depends on the provider and structure; understand reporting and contractual arrangements before applying.

Do I need property security? Not necessarily. Factoring is primarily based on receivables, though guarantees or security interests may apply.
