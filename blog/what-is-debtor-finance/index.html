What is debtor finance?

Zap Funds | 17 July 2026 | 6 min read

In short, Debt Finance turns the cash sitting idly in your unpaid invoices into working capital you can use today: Instead of waiting 30, 60 or 90 days for your customers to pay, the finance provider immediately advances you up to 90% of the invoice value, usually within 24 hours of it being processed, and only charges the remaining balance once your customers have received the money.

It goes by several names - invoice finance, invoice factoring, invoice discounting, receivables finance - but they all mean funding using the customer's ledger as security. Whatever it is named, it is nothing more complicated than that. Invoice as Security. Once your customer pays, the advance is settled. The more the customer invoices, the more it can fund.

Why businesses use debtor finance

Good businesses run into trouble as a result of the lag between finishing work and getting paid. Rent, tax, fuel, materials and wages are all due on their own payment schedules which might not align with your customers choosing to pay. Debtor finance can take those waiting invoices and turn them into working capital to make sure that payroll is covered and you are not falling behind on what you owe at the mercy of customers on their own terms.

No need to turn down work. Tight cash flows at companies cause them to turn down bigger contracts because the larger the job, the larger the up-front spending! With against the invoices funded, bigger contracts become an opportunity for growth, rather than a risky liability.

How debtor finance works

The cycle for each invoice is short once approved; it allows for cash to be quickly transferred into cash again by following the debtor paying procedure, being paid by the debtor again, and hence reducing the overall cash budget on the company, which can be put into better use; it creates higher business opportunities for expansion and reinvestments into the firm; by not giving discounts to large or loyal customers, it retains the firm.

  1. You complete work for a business customer and issue an invoice with agreed payment terms.
  2. You submit the invoice to the facility, usually through an online portal.
  3. The provider verifies the invoice and your customer, then advances up to 90% of its value, often within 24 hours.
  4. Your customer pays the invoice on their normal terms.
  5. The provider deducts its fees and releases the remaining balance to you.
  6. The available funds reset, ready for the next invoice.

You do not have to face any fixed repaying burden on cash flow between funding cycles, as the funding will simply be settled with money from payment from customers, rather than according to a schedule as what used to be the previous cases!

The two main types of debtor finance

Invoice factoring

The provider manages the collections - they notify your customers and collect the money from them directly. Best for those without any existing accounts team or those who want the chasing aspect dealt with for them.

Invoice discounting

Confidential - your customers just keep playing directly as normal and never know a finance company is involved. Suits established businesses that already do their own credit control.

Within both types you can also choose a whole ledger facility, where every eligible invoice flows through automatically, or a selective facility that funds single invoices when you need cash. Read more in our guide to invoice factoring.

A worked example

What is the example, then? This agency we mentioned earlier places temporary staff with a client. The agency pays its workers every week, but the client pays the agency on 30-day terms. Invoices are $50,000 for the month; this creates cash flow problems and means the company may have difficulty meeting its commitments unless it has sufficient cash available.

ItemAmount
Invoice value$50,000
Advance at 85%$42,500 within 24 hours
Balance until the client pays$7,500
Client pays on day 30$50,000
Balance released to you, minus fees$7,500

So it is another $42,500 that comes through this week, providing funds for payroll and placing more staff.

What debtor finance costs

Providers of invoice discounting/factoring charge fees, not interest. Fees in the main discount / funding fee (charged on the amount advanced and how long the invoice is outstanding). Administration fees (for the running of the facility) could be charged along with a one-off establishment fee when this is set up. They are calculated based on your sales, the credit worthiness and prompt payment of your customers, amongst others.

Always request a physical, written copy to confirm total costs are included and what will happen in respect of fees if the client is late with any payments.

Who debtor finance suits

Any business invoicing other businesses on payment terms. The industries we see most are transport and freight, recruitment and staffing, manufacturing, wholesale and distribution, cleaning and facilities, IT and professional services, engineering and trade services, and construction and trades.

Frequently asked questions about debtor finance

Is debtor finance the same as invoice finance?

Yes, debtor finance, invoice finance, factoring, and receivables finance are different names for funding secured against your unpaid customer invoices.

How much of an invoice can I access?

Providers advance normally 80-90% of the value of an eligible invoice, with the balance released when your customer pays, minus fees.

Do my customers need to know?

It is used for confidential arrangements; with invoice discounting, you still collect the money off the customer when the invoice falls due, but with factoring, you would tell your customers that factoring is in place.

Does debtor finance require property security?

For the majority, the answer is no. This is because the invoices themselves provide security, though businesses are known for taking up other guarantees or charges of business assets.

Can I fund single invoices instead of everything?

Yes, you contact the company only when required, and you draw down invoice funds when you need them to cover short-term cash flow shortages due to seasonal differences and non-recurring contracts.
