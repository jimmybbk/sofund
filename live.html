How Does Invoice Finance Work in Australia? A Step-by-Step Guide for Businesses

By Wayne Clothier | Zapfunds | 3-minute read

Running a business means paying bills long before some customers settle their invoices. Wages, materials, fuel, subcontractors and supplier bills all need attention, even when thousands of dollars sit in invoices.

For Australian businesses, this creates a frustrating gap between finishing work and getting paid.

Invoice finance is one way to manage that gap. Invoice finance allows eligible businesses to get money against customer invoices instead of waiting until the customer pays.

For businesses with 30, 60 or even 90‑day payment terms, getting that money earlier can make a difference to day‑to‑day operations.

In this guide, we will explain what invoice finance is, how invoice finance works, what invoice finance costs and when invoice finance may be worth considering.

What Is Invoice Finance?

Invoice finance is a type of business funding that lets a company get money that is tied up in customer invoices.

Of waiting for customers to settle their accounts, a business can receive an agreed advance against eligible invoices from a finance provider.

The remaining balance is usually released after the customer pays, the advance and applicable fees.

For example, imagine a construction company finishes a $50,000 project and issues an invoice with 60‑day payment terms.

The work is finished,. The business still needs to pay its subcontractors, buy materials and cover wages for the next job.

With an invoice finance facility, the company may be able to access part of that $50,000 before the customer pays.

The business can then use the funds to keep projects moving without relying on cash reserves.

In terms, invoice finance turns unpaid invoices into an earlier source of working capital.

How Does Invoice Finance Work?

Although the exact process varies between providers and facility types, invoice finance usually follows a process.

1. Your Business Completes the Work

Your business supplies goods or services to another business. Issues an invoice for payment.

The invoice contains agreed payment terms, such as 30, 60 or 90 days.

2. You Submit Eligible Invoices

You provide the invoices to your finance provider for assessment.

The provider may review the invoice amount, your customers creditworthiness, payment history and whether the underlying goods or services have been delivered.

3. You Receive an Advance

Once approved, the provider advances an agreed percentage of the invoice value.

The advance rate depends on the facility, the customer and the providers assessment.

4. Your Customer Pays the Invoice

Your customer pays according to the agreed payment terms.

Depending on the arrangement, payment may go directly to the finance provider. Be collected through your business.

5. The Remaining Balance Is Settled

Once payment is received, the invoice finance transaction is reconciled.

The remaining balance is released to your business after accounting for the advance and applicable fees.

What Types of Invoice Finance Are Available?

Invoice finance is not a product. Different arrangements suit businesses, depending on how they manage customer payments and accounts receivable.

Invoice Factoring

Invoice factoring involves assigning or selling invoices to a finance provider.

The provider generally takes responsibility for collecting payment from your customers.

This arrangement may suit businesses that want to reduce the work involved in chasing outstanding invoices.

Invoice Discounting

Invoice discounting allows a business to access funding against invoices while generally retaining responsibility for its own customer collections.

The business continues managing its accounts customer relationships.

Some arrangements can be confidential, meaning customers may not be told that invoice finance is being used. Availability depends on the provider and facility terms.

Selective Invoice Finance

Selective invoice finance allows a business to seek funding against invoices rather than necessarily financing its entire sales ledger.

This can be useful when a business has a large invoice that creates a temporary cash‑flow gap.

Not every provider offers facilities, so businesses should check the available options.

Why Do Australian Businesses Use Invoice Finance?

One of the challenges for growing businesses is that increased sales do not have to mean more cash in the bank.

A company might win new contracts, employ additional staff and purchase more materials, but still struggle to cover expenses while waiting for customers to pay.

Invoice finance can help address this timing problem.

Here are some reasons businesses consider invoice finance.

Managing Payroll

Employees and subcontractors expect to receive payment on time, no matter when customers settle their invoices.

Accessing funds against invoices may help businesses manage wages and contractor payments.

Paying Suppliers

Many businesses must buy materials, stock or equipment before they receive payment for completed work.

Invoice finance can provide working capital to cover these expenses.

Taking on New Contracts

Winning a contract can create extra costs before the first customer payment arrives.

Having access to funds tied up in existing invoices may help a business manage the expenses that come with growth.

Reducing Pressure on Cash Reserves

than using all available cash to cover operating expenses, a business may be able to access some of the money already owed to it.

This can give flexibility when managing day‑to‑day finances.

Which Industries Can Benefit From Invoice Finance?

Invoice finance is generally relevant to businesses that supply goods or services to businesses and issue invoices with payment terms.

Some industries where delayed customer payments can create working‑capital needs include:

  • Construction and building
  • Builders and contractors often pay subcontractors, suppliers and employees while waiting for progress claims and customer payments.
  • Transport and logistics
  • Transport operators need to cover fuel, vehicle maintenance, insurance and driver wages while waiting for freight customers to pay.
  • Manufacturing
  • Manufacturers may need to purchase raw materials, maintain production and pay staff before customers settle large orders.
  • Wholesale and distribution
  • Wholesalers can have substantial amounts of money tied up in stock and outstanding customer accounts.
  • Labour hire and recruitment
  • These businesses may need to pay employees weekly while clients operate on longer invoice payment terms.
  • Engineering and trade services
  • Businesses completing commercial projects may experience delays between finishing work, submitting invoices and receiving payment.

The suitability of invoice finance depends on the businesss invoicing arrangements, customer base and the finance providers eligibility requirements.

What Does Invoice Finance Cost?

Invoice finance providers generally charge fees for making funding available and managing the facility.

The total cost depends on the structure of the arrangement and the amount of time an invoice remains unpaid.

Common charges may include:

  • Discount or funding fees: Charges associated with the amount and the period the funding is outstanding.
  • Administration fees: Charges for managing the facility and processing invoices.
  • Establishment fees: Initial charges that may apply when setting up a facility.
  • Other applicable fees: Additional charges may apply depending on the agreement.

Before entering into an arrangement, businesses should ask for a breakdown of all costs.

It is also worth considering how fees change if customers take longer than expected to pay.

Comparing the cost of invoice finance against the value of having earlier access to cash can help determine whether it makes financial sense.

Invoice Finance vs Traditional Business Loans

Invoice finance and business loans can both provide working capital,. They operate differently.

Invoice financeTraditional Business Loan
Funding is linked to unpaid invoicesFunding is provided under a loan agreement
Advances depend on invoice eligibility and valueFunding depends on lender approval
Available funding may change as invoices are raised and paidFunding follows an agreed limit or loan amount
Repayment is generally connected to customer paymentsRepayments usually follow an agreed schedule
Security requirements depend on the facilitySecurity requirements vary by lender

Neither option is automatically better.

Invoice finance may be worth exploring when a business has reliable customers but struggles with the time between issuing invoices and receiving payment.

A traditional business loan may be more appropriate when a company needs an amount of funding for a longer‑term purchase or investment.

The right choice depends on the businesss circumstances, funding requirements and the total cost of borrowing.

What Should You Consider Before Applying?

Invoice finance can be useful,. It is not suitable for every business.

  • Your customer payment history: Businesses with established business customers and reliable payment records may be better positioned for assessment.
  • Your outstanding invoices: The quality, value and eligibility of your invoices can affect the amount of funding
  • Your profit margins: Funding costs need to be considered alongside the profitability of the work being financed.
  • Your existing finance arrangements: Existing security interests or funding agreements may affect your ability to establish another facility.
  • The terms of the agreement: Understand the fees, security requirements, collection arrangements and what happens if a customer fails to pay.

It is important to understand the agreement rather than focusing only on the advertised advance percentage.

Frequently Asked Questions About Invoice Finance

1. What is invoice finance in terms?

Invoice finance lets a business get money that is locked in customer invoices before the invoices are settled. Invoice finance gives working capital to help a business cover its expenses.

2. How much can I borrow against an invoice?

How much you can borrow against an invoice depends on the finance provider, the invoice itself and the facility agreement. Some providers may advance up to percent of an eligible invoice, although actual advance rates vary.

3. How quickly can invoice finance be arranged?

Timing differs between providers. After a facility is approved and invoices are verified, funding may be available quickly. New applications can take longer depending on the assessment process.

4. Does invoice finance require property security?

Not necessarily. Some invoice finance arrangements rely on business receivables, although providers may require additional security, guarantees or other arrangements.

5. Can small businesses use invoice finance?

Yes. Small businesses may qualify if they issue invoices to other businesses and meet the providers assessment requirements.

6. Do my customers know I am using invoice finance?

It depends on the arrangement. Factoring usually involves notifying customers, while some invoice discounting facilities may allow a business to keep control of collections.

7. What happens if my customer doesn't pay?

This depends on whether the facility's recourse or non‑recourse and on the terms of the agreement. Under recourse arrangements, the business may remain responsible for invoices.

8. Can I choose which invoices to finance?

Some providers offer invoice finance, allowing a business to fund individual invoices. Other facilities may require a portion of the sales ledger.

9. Is invoice finance the same as invoice factoring?

Not exactly. Invoice finance is the category of funding against outstanding invoices. Invoice factoring is one type of arrangement within invoice finance.

10. Is invoice finance suitable for growing businesses?

It can be. Businesses experiencing increased sales may also see amounts of unpaid invoices. Invoice finance may help provide working capital while customers take time to pay.
